How to Tell If Amber Is Real: 7 Simple Tests

Salt Water · Warmth · Static · UV Light · Inclusions · Red Flags · Buying

Real Baltic amber is a fossilised tree resin tens of millions of years old — warm, light, and full of natural character. Because it is prized and valuable, it is also widely imitated with copal, plastic, and glass. The good news is that genuine amber behaves in ways that fakes cannot easily copy. This guide walks through seven simple, mostly non-destructive tests you can do at home, explains the red flags to watch for when buying, and shows how a certificate of authenticity gives you the certainty that no single test can.

Why Amber Authenticity Matters

Genuine Baltic amber, known to scientists as succinite, is fossilised conifer resin that formed around the Baltic region roughly 40 million years ago. Its value comes from that great age, its warm natural colour, and the tiny inclusions of ancient air and plant matter locked inside. An imitation may look similar at a glance, but it carries none of that history — and often a fraction of the value.

The market is full of look-alikes. Some are honest substitutes clearly labelled as resin or reconstituted amber; others are sold as the real thing at real-amber prices. Learning to tell the difference protects both your money and your trust in what you wear. The tests below are the same principles gemologists rely on, simplified for home use. Use two or three together — no single test is proof on its own, but a piece that passes several with flying colours is almost certainly genuine amber.

What Fake Amber Is Usually Made Of

Before testing, it helps to know what you are testing against. Most amber imitations fall into four groups, and each behaves differently.

  • Copal — young, immature resin that has not fully fossilised. It looks remarkably like amber and even floats in salt water, but it is far softer, has a lower melting point, and dissolves more easily in solvents. Copal is the trickiest imitation because it is genuine resin, just millions of years too young.
  • Plastic — bakelite, celluloid, acrylic, and modern polyester resins are moulded to mimic amber. They often feel subtly heavier or harder, may smell of chemicals or burnt plastic when heated, and rarely show convincing natural inclusions.
  • Glass — heavy, cold to the touch, and impossible to scratch with a fingernail. Glass does not build a static charge and sinks immediately in salt water, making it one of the easiest fakes to spot.
  • Pressed or reconstituted amber — real amber fragments melted and compressed into a single block. It is technically amber, but far less valuable than one natural piece, and often shows cloudy flow lines or an unnaturally uniform colour.

The 7 Tests: How to Tell If Amber Is Real

1. The Salt Water Test

This is the classic and most reliable non-destructive test. Dissolve about two tablespoons of salt in a cup of warm water, stirring until no more salt dissolves. Gently drop your amber in. Because genuine Baltic amber is only slightly denser than saturated salt water, it floats or hovers just below the surface. Plastics and glass are denser and sink. The one caveat: copal floats too, so a floating piece still needs the warmth and static checks to be confirmed. Rinse and dry the piece afterwards, especially if it is set in silver.

2. The Warmth & Touch Test

Amber is an organic material and a poor conductor of heat, so it warms quickly against your skin and never feels cold and heavy the way glass does. Hold the piece in your closed hand for a minute. Real amber feels warm and light, almost weightless for its size. Glass stays cool and feels dense; many plastics warm up but feel harder and less alive. This test costs nothing and is a good first impression before you set up the salt water.

3. The Static Electricity Test

The ancient Greeks called amber elektron because of this very property — it is the root of the word electricity. Rub the amber briskly against a piece of wool, cotton, or your hair for around 30 seconds, then hold it close to a few small torn scraps of paper or a little dust. Genuine amber builds a static charge and attracts the light material. Most plastics generate weaker static and glass generates almost none, so a strong pull is a good sign of authenticity.

4. The UV Light Test

Under a UV or blacklight, genuine Baltic amber fluoresces — it typically glows a pale blue or milky blue-green, especially on an uncut surface. Copal shows a much weaker glow, and most plastics either stay dull or fluoresce a flat white. A small UV torch is inexpensive and makes this one of the more telling checks, though results vary with the amber's colour and finish, so read it alongside the other tests rather than on its own.

5. The Inclusions & Look Test

Hold the piece to the light and look closely, ideally with a loupe or magnifier. Natural amber often contains tiny bubbles, plant fragments, cracks, or subtle variations in colour. These small imperfections are a hallmark of the real thing. Imitations, by contrast, tend to look too perfect — flawlessly clear, uniformly coloured, or dotted with suspiciously large, dramatic "insects" placed to impress. Perfectly identical beads across an entire strand are a warning sign of moulded plastic or pressed amber.

6. The Scratch & Hardness Test

Amber is soft but not as soft as copal or many plastics. Pressed gently, a fingernail will not scratch genuine amber, while immature copal may mark more easily. This test is more useful for what it rules out than what it confirms, and it should be done cautiously on an inconspicuous spot — the goal is a light press, never a gouge that would damage a finished piece.

7. The Hot Needle & Smell Test

This is the one destructive test, so use it only on raw material or where a tiny mark will not matter — never on a finished piece of jewellery. A needle heated and touched to the surface makes genuine amber release a faint piney, resinous scent. Copal smells sweeter and softens quickly. Plastic gives off a sharp, acrid chemical smell of burning synthetic. Because it leaves a mark, most people skip this test on jewellery and rely on the six non-destructive checks above.

Red Flags When Buying Amber

Tests are useful once a piece is in your hands, but many problems can be spotted before you buy. Watch for these warning signs.

  • A price too good to be true. Genuine natural amber cut and set by hand is not a bargain-bin material. Very cheap "amber" beads are usually plastic, copal, or pressed amber.
  • Flawless, identical pieces. A whole strand of beads matched perfectly in colour and clarity is a strong sign of moulding rather than natural stone.
  • Dramatic, oversized insects. Genuine inclusions are usually small and subtle; large, showy creatures perfectly centred in clear resin are often planted in plastic.
  • Vague sourcing. A seller who cannot say where the amber comes from, or who avoids the words "natural" and "Baltic," should give you pause.
  • No certificate. Reputable sellers provide a certificate of authenticity confirming natural Baltic origin. Its absence is the biggest red flag of all.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I tell if amber is real at home?
The simplest home test is the salt water test: dissolve about two tablespoons of salt in a cup of warm water until saturated, then drop the amber in. Genuine Baltic amber is very light and floats or hovers, while most plastic and glass imitations sink. Real amber also warms quickly against the skin, feels light rather than heavy like glass, builds a static charge when rubbed against wool or cotton, and often contains tiny natural bubbles or inclusions. No single test is conclusive, so combine two or three for confidence, and remember that a certificate of authenticity from a trusted seller is the most reliable proof.
What is fake amber usually made of?
Fake amber is most often made from copal, plastic, or glass. Copal is young, immature resin that resembles amber but has not fully fossilised, so it is much softer and can be sold as amber by mistake or intent. Plastics such as bakelite, celluloid, and modern polyester resin are moulded to look like amber but feel different and behave differently in tests. Glass imitations are heavy and cold to the touch and do not build static. Pressed or reconstituted amber, made from melted amber fragments, is technically amber but far less valuable than a single natural piece.
Does real amber float in salt water?
Yes. Genuine Baltic amber has a density close to that of saturated salt water, so it floats or hovers just below the surface while denser materials sink. To run the test, stir about two tablespoons of salt into a cup of warm water until no more dissolves, then gently drop the piece in. Amber and copal both float, while most plastics and all glass imitations sink to the bottom. The salt water test is one of the most reliable non-destructive checks, but pair it with the warmth and static tests, since copal floats too and needs further checks to distinguish from true amber.
Is my amber jewelry real if it was cheap?
A very low price is one of the strongest warning signs of fake or reconstituted amber. Genuine natural Baltic amber is a fossilised material tens of millions of years old, cut and set by hand, so authentic pieces command a fair price. Mass-produced beads sold for a few dollars are usually plastic, copal, or pressed amber rather than a single natural stone. Price alone is not proof either way, but bargain amber that looks perfectly uniform, flawless, and identical across every bead deserves careful testing. Always buy from a seller who provides a certificate of authenticity confirming natural Baltic origin.
